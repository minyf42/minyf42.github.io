Why these reading options?

What the research says about text size, spacing, line width, and font, and why readers should be able to choose.

The essay Missed by the Big Picture lets you change how its text looks. This page explains why each option is there.

The short answer: no single way of setting text is best for everyone. In a large online study with hundreds of readers, the fastest font differed from person to person, and the gap between a reader’s fastest and slowest font was about 35% in reading speed (Wallace et al., 2022). The same idea runs through my research: before we can tell which presentation of a text serves a reader, we have to know where that reader’s processing gets stuck. Until a page can work that out for you, the next best thing is to let you choose.

Text size

Larger text is not only for people with low vision. In an eye-tracking study of reading on screens, larger font sizes improved both readability and comprehension (Rello et al., 2016). The default here is 18px, and you can go up to 26px.

Line spacing

Space between lines helps the eye find the start of the next line, which matters most when lines are long or when a reader tends to skip lines. The evidence is weaker than for text size: in the same study, line spacing had a smaller effect than font size (Rello et al., 2016). It is offered here because some readers find a looser page much easier, and it costs nothing to try.

Letter spacing

This option has the strongest evidence. When letters sit close together, each one makes its neighbors harder to recognize, an effect called visual crowding. In a large, unselected sample of Italian and French children with dyslexia, extra-large letter spacing substantially improved text reading on the spot, with no training at all (Zorzi et al., 2012). The benefit is not universal, though. In a study from Jason Yeatman’s lab, the benefit of increased letter, word, and line spacing showed up in a sub-group of adults with dyslexia who had elevated crowding (Joo et al., 2018). That is exactly why it is a choice and not the default. “Wide” and “Wider” also increase the space between words in step.

Line width

Very long lines make it hard to find the next line; very short lines interrupt reading with constant line breaks. On screens, one study found that longer lines could be read faster, while moderate line lengths supported comprehension better (Dyson & Haselgrove, 2001). Typographers usually recommend about 60 to 80 characters per line. “Narrow” and “Medium” fall in or near that range; “Full” uses the width of the page.

Font

You may have heard of fonts designed for dyslexia, such as OpenDyslexic or Dyslexie. Controlled studies have not found that they improve reading speed or accuracy (Wery & Diliberto, 2017; Kuster et al., 2018). Whether a font has serifs also matters less than people think (Arditi & Cho, 2005). What does seem to matter is the individual reader (Wallace et al., 2022). So the page offers two clear options: Source Serif, and Atkinson Hyperlegible, a sans-serif typeface designed by the Braille Institute so that easily confused characters, such as I, l, and 1, look distinct.

Light and dark

The moon icon in the menu bar switches between a light and a dark page. Some readers find dark backgrounds easier on the eyes; others read better with dark text on a light page. Use whichever feels better.

Your choices are saved

Your settings are stored only in your own browser, so they will still be there the next time you open the essay. The ↺ button returns everything to the defaults.
