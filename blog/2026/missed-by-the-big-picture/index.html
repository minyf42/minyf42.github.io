他（卡尔·刘易斯）知道奥林匹斯山上的神火为何而燃烧，那不是为了一个人把另一个人打败，而是为了有机会向诸神炫耀人类的不屈，命定的局限尽可永在，不屈的挑战却不可须臾或缺。

“He [Carl Lewis] knew why the sacred fire on Mount Olympus burns: not so that one person may defeat another, but so that humankind may show the gods its refusal to yield. The limits fate has set may last forever, but the unyielding challenge to them must never lapse, not even for a moment.”

— 史铁生《我的梦想》 (Shi Tiesheng, My Dream; my own rough translation)

One autistic woman I interviewed could not follow lectures in college. The lectures moved faster than she could assemble the logic, and no one suggested that she might simply process information differently. She concluded that she was not smart enough. So she taught herself, top-down from an outline and at her own pace, and she has now spent more than five years in one of the most competitive fields in finance. She found her route. What stays with me is how much of it she had to find alone. Others I interviewed had less room to find theirs; several changed jobs almost every year.

Accounts come from pilot interviews conducted in Fall 2026 and from women I know in the community, ahead of a full ethnographic study planned for Spring 2027. They are shared with permission; some are combined, and identifying details have been changed.

Missed by a stereotype

Autism and dyslexia are both identified through a picture of what the difficulty looks like, and for decades that picture has been a boy’s. Pooled across studies, about four autistic boys are diagnosed for every girl, although the true ratio is likely closer to three to one (Loomes et al., 2017); among autistic people without intellectual disability, the reported gap has been wider still, as high as eight to one (Fombonne, 2003, as cited in Ratto et al., 2018). Dyslexia shows the same pattern at the point of referral. When one longitudinal study tested every child with the same measures, boys and girls did not differ significantly in reading disability, yet schools had identified 13.6% of second-grade boys and only 3.2% of girls (Shaywitz et al., 1990). Boys may truly be somewhat more likely to have reading disability (Rutter et al., 2004), but the gap in who gets noticed has been wider than the gap in who struggles.

Part of the reason is how girls are socialized to handle difficulty. They are expected to fit in, to keep the peace, and to look inside themselves for the cause of any mismatch. Many learn to quiet exactly the signs a diagnosis looks for. The old picture fits trans and nonbinary autistic people even less, and gender diversity is more common among autistic people than in the general population (Warrier et al., 2020). For years before I learned I was dyslexic, when my eyes skipped lines on a page, I blamed myself for being restless and careless, and I was too embarrassed to tell anyone that reading was hard. It did not occur to me that the difficulty had a name.

A cost paid more than once

The stereotype fails the people it leaves out more than once. First it withholds a diagnosis, and without a diagnosis there is no support, only more hours spent compensating, until the effort itself turns into burnout. It is Sisyphean work: the effort must be paid again each day, and none of it makes the hill any lower. Then, even when a woman has worked out exactly where she needs an accommodation, her request is more likely to be dismissed as a lazy excuse, because she does not look the way the stereotype says an autistic or dyslexic person should. Being recognized is no safer. In two cases I know closely, the story ran the same way. Each woman was placed on a performance plan at work. The strain led to depression, and in seeking help, she learned for the first time that she was autistic. Each realized that the problems the plan listed were exactly the ones accommodations could address, disclosed her diagnosis, and asked for support. Both were told the company would not have hired them had it known, and both left within months.

The same pattern appears at scale. In research with Prof. Julia Hur on U.S. workplace discrimination charges, we found that discrimination against people with cognitive disabilities is among the hardest for complaint systems to recognize, in part because others assume such a disability could be overcome with more effort. It appears in machines too: when I told language models that a reader was autistic, their explanations became 25 to 30% shorter while still passing as more readable. People and models alike respond to the label, not to the person.

Sociologists have a precise account of how this happens. Bruce Link and Jo Phelan describe stigma as a process that unfolds in steps: a difference is labeled, the label is linked to a stereotype, the person is set apart, and they lose status and face discrimination (Link & Phelan, 2001). Many of the women I met had been through every step. Damian Milton adds that what gets labeled is often not a deficit at all. His double empathy problem holds that communication between autistic and non-autistic people breaks down in both directions, because each side struggles to read the other; yet only one side is held responsible for the misunderstanding (Milton, 2012).

A smaller safety margin

My own boulder was lighter: my family could afford tutors, and I had strengths that let me compensate for years. For women with a smaller safety margin, the same climb can be unbearable.

What can be controlled, and what cannot

Among neurodivergent people, in interviews and on online forums alike, one question returns again and again: Should I go back to school? To work? To a relationship? The replies are usually goal-oriented: list the pros and cons, make a plan, push through. I have come to think a different question should come first: how much of this is within one’s control, and how much is not?

Some of it is. A person can learn their limits, budget their energy, and take time alone before burnout rather than after it. But some of it is not: a text that will not come together however many times they read it, or the stigma that leads many neurodivergent people to camouflage, wary that their traits will be read as flaws of character. No amount of advice reaches that second part. This is the core of the social model of disability: much of what disables a person lies not in their body or mind but in how the world around them is built (Oliver, 1990). That is where tools should do the work, so that a person’s effort is not spent compensating for barriers the environment created. It is why I am now building a scheduler that plans around cognitive load, after building a tool that locates where meaning diverges and finding that it could not do what I had hoped.

When what cannot be controlled accumulates

The stakes are not abstract. A Swedish registry study of more than 27,000 autistic people found that they died on average about 16 years earlier than matched controls (mean age at death 53.9 versus 70.2 years), and that their risk of death by suicide was about 7.5 times higher. Suicide was the only cause of death more elevated in autistic people without intellectual disability than in those with it, and its relative risk was higher for women than for men. Those who died by suicide had been diagnosed, on average, less than three years earlier, and diagnosis in this sample often came late (Hirvikoski et al., 2016). A Danish national cohort found a similar sex difference in suicide attempts: autistic women attempted suicide at 4.4 times the rate of autistic men, compared with 1.4 times among non-autistic people (Kõlves et al., 2021).

Behind these numbers is rarely a single crisis. More often it is attrition: the course dropped, the job lost, the accommodation denied. Sociologists call this kind of disadvantage cumulative: each setback makes the next more likely, so small early gaps widen over a lifetime (Merton, 1968; DiPrete & Eirich, 2006). More than a century ago, Émile Durkheim argued that suicide rates are social facts, rising when people are poorly integrated into the groups around them or when the norms that should guide them break down (Durkheim, 1897/1951). That loss is not inevitable. Research points to conditions that can be changed, and much of this risk is preventable.

Michel Foucault described modern power as the power to “make live and let die”: it manages populations, directs care toward the norm, and lets those outside the norm fall away (Foucault, 2003). No single decision excludes these women. They are left out of how classrooms, workplaces, and assessments are designed.

If you are struggling or thinking about suicide, you do not have to carry it alone. In the U.S., call or text 988; in mainland China, call 12356; elsewhere, find a local line at findahelpline.com.

Pain without words

Their pain also goes unheard for a quieter reason. Miranda Fricker calls it hermeneutical injustice: when a group lacks the shared concepts to make its experience intelligible, its members cannot fully explain what is happening to them, sometimes not even to themselves (Fricker, 2007). One of her central examples is Wendy Sanford, who in the late 1960s experienced what we now call postnatal depression; with no name for it, neither she nor her husband could see it as a condition that needed help. I recognize that silence from the years before I knew I was dyslexic. When a difficulty has no name, the person who has it is the first to be blamed for it, often by themselves.

A new picture

Autistic people are often said to miss the big picture, to see the trees but not the forest, although this attention to detail is better understood as a cognitive style than a deficit (Happé & Frith, 2006). The women I met suggest the reverse: it is the big picture, built from averages and from studies of mostly boys, that misses them, and misses the details they notice. If the old picture of who struggles hides these women, then part of the work is to draw a new one. Ian Hacking showed that classifications of people are not passive descriptions: the people classified respond to them, and in responding they change what the category means (Hacking, 1995). A category can be remade from the inside. I want to help draw a more accurate picture of neurodivergent women, one that includes the many ways they already work well, often in ways the default does not anticipate. My research tries to do this from three sides. Better assessment can make their differences visible before they are mistaken for deficits; assistive tools can carry part of what cannot be controlled; and ethnography can give these women’s experiences the words they have been missing.

Imagine Sisyphus happy

Sisyphus is remembered for the boulder, but less often for why he was given it: he had once chained Death, so that for a time no one died. The work I care about happens long before life and death are at stake: in the classrooms, assessments, and workplaces where people are first given up on.

Camus asks us to imagine Sisyphus happy. I can, as long as each push leaves footprints on ground that had no path, so that the next woman the picture misses has a direction: a path to follow, or a pitfall to avoid. I hope she steps over my struggles, reaches higher than I could, and one day escapes the loop.
