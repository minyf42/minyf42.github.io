*Ethnographic study · Fall 2026 – Present*

Autistic women are often diagnosed late, and many spend their careers masking: managing how they come across, deciding whether to disclose, and absorbing the effort that small talk and indirect communication demand. Research on their working lives remains thin, especially beyond Western, English-speaking settings.

The project began as a pilot in User Research Fundamentals at NYU Shanghai, where I conducted semi-structured interviews with three autistic women professionals. Recurring themes centered on access to learning at work and on keeping a job over time. With Xiaomian Zhu (Duke Kunshan University, Sociology ’27), I’m now developing the pilot into a full ethnographic study, with interviews planned for Spring 2027 pending IRB approval.

The design pairs two perspectives. Institutional ethnography (Smith) looks at how workplaces organize experience: for example, how an accommodation request is recast as a “personal need.” Goffman’s work on stigma and Hochschild’s on emotional labor capture the cost to individuals, treating masking and disclosure as unpaid interactional work. Intersectionality (Crenshaw) runs across both, since gender, autism, migration and language status compound. The study complements my research on disability discrimination charges with [Prof. Julia Hur](https://juliadhur.com/lab/), which asks the same question at scale: whether institutions can recognize harm that is less visible.
