**Working paper with [Prof. Julia D. Hur](https://juliadhur.com/lab/) (NYU Stern).**

Workers with neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions face high rates of job loss, yet whether their discrimination claims are recognized as discrimination has received little systematic attention. Research on disability discrimination has largely treated disability as a single category, overlooking that some conditions, such as autism, learning disability, depression, and schizophrenia, show up mainly in behavior rather than in the body (Weiner, Perry, & Magnusson, 1988). This paper asks whether discrimination against workers with these mental disabilities is less likely to be recognized than discrimination against workers with physical disabilities. Using 324,519 disability discrimination charges filed with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission from fiscal years 2011 to 2017, we estimate charge-level logistic regressions of investigators’ determinations. Conditions are classified with the Social Security Administration’s impairment listings, and the models control for the issue charged, employer characteristics, year, and state. Compared with charges based on physical disabilities, charges based on neurodevelopmental conditions are more likely to be closed with a finding of no cause (OR = 1.20) and less likely to result in a finding of cause (OR = 0.72). Charges based on psychiatric conditions show the same pattern (OR = 1.12 and 0.63), and the results hold across multiple robustness checks. Exploratory analyses show that the more a condition disrupts social interaction, the more often its charges are closed with no cause. Clinical severity and the public’s image of disability do not track outcomes in the same way. These findings point to an attributional account: when a disability looks like misconduct, discrimination against it may look like legitimate performance management. They identify an overlooked boundary in how organizations and enforcement agencies recognize discrimination.

Study 2, an experiment testing the attributional mechanism, is in preparation.
