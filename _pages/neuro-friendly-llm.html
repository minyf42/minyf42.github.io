When a language model is told the reader is autistic, its answers score better on readability and cover less of the content. The accessibility measures in use today can’t see the difference, because they only look at words and sentences.

Minyue Fan · Pre-doctoral Research Assistant, NYU Shanghai · Faculty supporter: Prof. Hua Shen

Why this matters

The measures validated for judging text accessibility for autistic readers work at the level of words and sentences: word frequency, sentence length, overlap between neighbouring sentences. None of them checks whether a passage still says everything it needs to, or whether the links between ideas are stated or left for the reader to fill in.

So a model can be rewarded for text that looks easier and says less. That is a fairness problem. The reader who disclosed being autistic gets the thinner answer, and the scores tell everyone it was the better one.

First result: simpler style, thinner content

I asked five models the same 32 explanation questions under three prompts: a neutral prompt, one saying the reader is autistic, and one asking for plain, explicit writing without mentioning autism. Each answer was scored for readability and for how many of the question’s expected key points it covered.

Mean over 32 questions per prompt for each model (96 answers per model). Pro was collected through the Gemini web app, not the API. Coverage uses a provisional word-matching measure; human annotation is in progress.

  • In all five models, the autism prompt raised readability by 10 to 36 points and lowered key-point coverage by 8 to 31 percentage points. More answers were flagged as under-explained.
  • The plain-and-explicit prompt barely moved readability. So the jump in “easy” scores comes with the autism label, not with a request for clear writing.
  • The open-weight 8B models (Llama 3.1, Qwen3) show the largest shifts. The Gemini models show the same direction, with smaller coverage losses.

Groundwork: how AI text differs from human text

Before the prompt study, I compared human and AI answers to the same questions in two public corpora (HC3, about 23,000 question pairs; M4, across Reddit, Wikipedia, WikiHow and arXiv).

  • ChatGPT-style answers have deeper, more nested sentences and more subordinate clauses than human answers to the same question.
  • They use more scalar words like “some”, “often” and “most”, which leave the exact meaning for the reader to infer.
  • Older or smaller models (Flan-T5, Dolly, Cohere) often go the other way, so “AI text” is not one style.

What I’m not claiming yet

  • Readability scores are surface measures. Higher scores don’t show that a text is easier for autistic readers, and lower coverage doesn’t yet show they understand less. The reading study is what will test that.
  • Coverage is currently measured by word matching against expected key points, which can miss paraphrases.
  • Gemini Pro answers came from the web app, where about a third included images, tables or interactive elements that weren’t scored.
